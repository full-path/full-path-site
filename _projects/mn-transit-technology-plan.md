By 2021, transit agencies across Greater Minnesota were under the same pressure showing up nationally: mobile computing, data-intensive vendor products, and rider expectations were pushing agencies of every size toward more sophisticated technology, faster than most had the organizational capacity to absorb. Small and rural providers in particular were acquiring onboard systems and software they couldn't fully support once the sales process ended — no IT staff to keep a tablet fleet patched, no documented procedures for the data a new system produced, no consistent way to weigh what a vendor promised against what it would actually take to run. The Minnesota Department of Transportation commissioned this plan to give Greater Minnesota's transit agencies, and MnDOT itself, a structured way to think about technology investment as an organizational capacity problem, not just a procurement decision.

Full Path's primary contribution was Chapter 4, Technology and Management Solutions, and within it the "Organizational Supports for Technology" section — a map of the support functions a technology program actually depends on, including vendor support, training, documentation, access to technology domain experts, cybersecurity, system design and planning, grant management, system testing, procurement, and evaluation of outcomes, along with how those functions reinforce one another. Full Path also developed the Transit Technology Flowcharts, which lay out how core technology systems relate to each other differently at small, medium, and large agencies, giving each size tier a concrete starting map rather than a one-size-fits-all system diagram. A third contribution, the centralized-versus-distributed framework, gave the plan a vocabulary for a question every state DOT eventually faces: which technology functions — leadership, market research, procurement, training — are better handled at the state level, and which should stay with individual transit agencies. The framework illustrated this with contrasting profiles: a typical state's largely distributed model, and Oregon DOT's more centralized one.

The plan's lasting contribution was reframing technology adoption around organizational readiness rather than feature comparison — a distinction that shows up throughout Full Path's later work. The flowcharts and the centralized-versus-distributed framework, in particular, gave MnDOT and other state DOTs reusable tools for a conversation most had been having informally at best: how much technology support agencies can realistically provide for themselves, and what that means for what the state should take on instead.
