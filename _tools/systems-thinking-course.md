Most people who end up responsible for transit technology arrive with experience using consumer software, not with any background in how technology functions inside an organization. There's no certification path that teaches this, so it's common to overestimate what a piece of software can do on its own and underestimate the organizational work — training, documentation, stakeholder buy-in — that determines whether it actually succeeds. This course was originally developed for the National Center for Mobility Management, and moved to the CCAM Technical Assistance Center after NCMM shuttered. It teaches transit agency staff to close that gap using systems thinking: a way of looking at an organization's technology as a set of interconnected parts, rather than a series of standalone purchases.

The eight modules build a single toolkit step by step. Early modules cover foundational concepts — a "transit stack" for inventorying an agency's technology systems, and the organizational supports (training, documentation, vendor support, cybersecurity, and more) that determine whether those systems actually work. From there, the course moves into stakeholder engagement and responsibility assignment matrices, then into visualizing data flows and mapping the "journey" data takes through an organization. The back half turns to problem-solving directly: identifying pain points, writing clear problem statements, ranking them by probability and impact, and generating solutions across different types of leverage points — physical changes, information flows, system structures, and mental models — before closing with project planning, testing, evaluation, and vendor procurement.

Throughout, the course leans on worked examples and small case studies of fictional transit agencies to make each tool concrete, and each module builds on homework from the last, so the frameworks accumulate into a single practical approach rather than staying abstract. It's aimed squarely at people without a technology background, and its guiding idea is a simple one: agencies get better outcomes from technology by understanding their own problems clearly first, rather than starting with a solution and working backward.
